It is fascinating how English has, over time, become a common language with more than 
a billion people using the language to converse, all this tied to globalisation, which has expanded 
and also changed English influence and function in the 21st century (Hassan, 2021). English has 
transitioned from colonisation use and spread to the integration of fields in science, academics, 
and international organisations. Bozhko et al. (2024) posit that globalisation is the rapid cross-
border exchange of politics, culture, society and information, which creates interconnectedness 
among these countries. The rise of digital platforms such as social media has accelerated the rate 
of globalisation and hence enhanced the spread, use and change of English meaning all over the 
world in a short period of time (Bozhko et al. 2024). Therefore, globalisation has influenced 
enhancing English status as an international language, caused variations in the use of semantics, 
but has also resulted in English becoming an imperialist language.   
Globalisation has significantly influenced the original status of English as a colonial 
language to an international language, hence making it a bridge of communication between 
people of different cultures. Nafees and Farid (2025) state that English is more than a medium in 
diplomacy and global business, relating it to cross-cultural communications between cultures 
from different communities. These authors have a strong conviction that countries' cooperation 
and harmony, as an aspect of globalisation, have been enhanced with increased use of English. 
Not only does globalisation result in to spread of standardised English, but also influences and 
encourages localisation, as explained by Zhang and Su (2021) to be the use of English in local 
cultural contexts. In addition to the spread and use in different cultural contexts, English has 
formed significant foundations in the field of global technology, advancing in production of 
usage terms and scientific naming across the globe (Choriyevna, 2025). This proves that the 
English status of a British origin and colonial language has changed abruptly to fit in the global 
context of communications in diplomacy, business, cross-cultural interactions and global 
technology.  
In addition, as globalisation plays its course in exposing people to English through social 
media every day, the use of semantics starts to vary across different cultural contexts, leading to 
new informal language varieties. Rahmani and Karimi (2025) explain how social media 
platforms such as Facebook and Instagram have resulted in to increase use of slang as an 
addition to formal English. This effect happens as a result of shortening words, when sending 


messages, and borrowing of cultural words to explain experiences, which end up being 
integrated into the regular use of English. Therefore, the people brought together by this platform 
find themselves accustomed to this informal English, where, for instance, instead of saying the 
front camera picture, it becomes global and normal to say “selfie”, while the shortening of 
phrases such as “laugh out loud” turns to be “LOL” (Lehkonen, 2025). This author also states 
that social media as a tool of globalisation, integrates cultural linguistic features, which 
diversifies the use of English in daily activities, and also results in variations in syntax and 
accent.  
However, the amplification of English through globalisation has posed a challenge to 
other native languages, which might become extinct with the cultures that they embed. Zeng et 
al. (2023) state that people use English as a source of prestige and as a basic need to gain more 
social and economic opportunities. This risk is the languages of the ancient cultures, which, with 
consistent globalisation, might end up being forgotten. Hall (2020) states that languages carry 
cultural representation, and when they are not used, cultural aspects get forgotten too. 
Globalisation has positioned English as a necessity to survive in the employment, education and 
global sector, which makes English become an imperial language, marginalising the local 
languages. Through the integration of English into schools, primary publishing language for 
books, articles and journals, and in international organisations, members around these institutions 
unwillingly forget and are unable to use their native languages.  
In conclusion, globalisation influences English’s meaning and use through changes of 
context to an international language, new uses of semantics through social media and also 
imperialism over other local languages. Countries need to balance the spread, use and meaning 
of English as they might lose their cultural identity if their people only prioritise English. This 
could be by having subjects that are taught in pure native languages and also enhancing cultural 
education, expression and identity.
